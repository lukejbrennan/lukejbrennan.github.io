The joy summer brings is so expansive and lovely! And whenever winter starts peaking around the corner, I remember it can be sort of dark and depressing.

But what can ya do.

Light some long-ass* candles I guess.

Your mood sort of makes your reality. Living in a mood can be like living in wet pants. Change your pants? Change your life.

When I was a kid, I remember wishing that summer would never end. Or that every day could be Christmas. Sort of problematic mentality. Definitely not possible.

I still have that impulse today: I find myself wishing I could speed through different moments (dishes, laundry, work) so that I could just get to the good stuff.

Le creme de cremes.

And that’s not even to mention getting _out_ of the actual hard stuff. Pain / suffering / grief — the _for-the-love-of-God-get-me-out-of-here_ kinds of moments. So how do you integrate those moments (which make up life, which are going to happen), into a good life?

Stephen Colbert and Anderson Cooper talk about this question in [an interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YB46h1koicQ): “You told an interviewer that you have learned to, in your words, ‘Love the thing I most wish had not happened.’ You went on to say, what punishments from God are not gifts. Do you really believe that?”

Stephen Colbert (hero) argues “It’s a gift to exist. And with existence comes suffering.”

No way out of it. His whole idea is that you can’t be grateful for certain parts of your life, but you have to be grateful for all of it.

This argument isn’t that satisfying or convincing to me. I definitely have the _experience_ of being only grateful for parts of my life.

But then he makes an argument that you can find utility, things to be grateful, some sense of meaning in suffering. “Well what do you get from loss? You get awareness of other people’s loss, which allows you to connect with that other person, which allows you to love more deeply, and to understand what it’s like to be a human being...”

And that feels nice. To think that… we’re not suffering for nothing.

It also reminds me of this bit from [Viktor Frankl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logotherapy):

> “Once, an elderly general practitioner consulted me because of his severe depression. He could not overcome the loss of his wife who had died two years before and whom he had loved above all else. Now how could I help him? What should I tell him? I refrained from telling him anything, but instead confronted him with a question, “What would have happened, Doctor, if you had died first, and your wife would have had to survive without you?” “Oh,” he said, “for her this would have been terrible; how she would have suffered!” Whereupon I replied, “You see, Doctor, such a suffering has been spared her, and it is you who have spared her this suffering; but now, you have to pay for it by surviving and mourning her.” He said no word but shook my hand and calmly left the office.3(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logotherapy#cite_note-Frankl2006-3): 178–179
